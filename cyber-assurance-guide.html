What this module actually does

Cybersecurity Assurance answers one question for an auditor or an executive: do you know what you own, do you know what's wrong with it, and can you prove you did something about it?

Everything in this module exists to support that sentence. There are three moving parts and they are deliberately separate:

PartAnswersLives at
IT Asset InventoryWhat do we own, who owns it, how much does it matter?/assets
VulnerabilitiesWhat is wrong with those things, and how urgent is each one?/vulnerabilities
Risk PosturePutting both together — how exposed is this asset, overall?/risk-posture
Why a business reader should care

A vulnerability scanner tells you there are 4,000 problems. That is not useful to anyone who has to decide what to fund. This module's job is to turn 4,000 findings into an ordered list with owners, deadlines and evidence — and to be able to show, months later, why a particular decision was made and who made it.

How to read this guide

Each page explains three things: what you see, where every number comes from, and why it exists at all. If a number is derived, assumed or borrowed, this guide says so — that distinction matters more than the number itself.

The mental model

Three objects, two relationships. Everything else is presentation.

Asset←— Vulnerability—→ Risk Posture

1. An asset is a thing you own

A server, a laptop, a database, an application. It has an owner, a criticality, and a set of properties that describe how much damage its loss would cause. Assets are the anchor — a vulnerability with no asset is an abstract fact about software, not a risk to your business.

2. A vulnerability is a defect found on one or more assets

Usually a CVE (a publicly catalogued flaw). The same CVE can affect many assets, and one asset can carry many CVEs. That's a many-to-many link, and it's why "how bad is this CVE" and "how bad is this for us" are different questions.

3. Risk posture is the asset's overall exposure

It blends five signals — benchmark compliance, open vulnerabilities, data sensitivity, control coverage, and linked risks — into one 0–100 score. It is the only number that tries to summarise an asset in a single figure.

The distinction that trips everyone up

Vulnerability priority (0–100) ranks findings against each other so you know what to fix first. Risk posture (0–100) rates an asset. They are different numbers on different scales measuring different things, and they will not match. That is correct, not a bug.

How a change travels

Change one thing and it should move everywhere downstream. This is the chain:

You changeIt movesBecause
Asset criticalityVulnerability priority on that asset · asset postureCriticality is 10% of vulnerability priority and feeds the CIA dimension
Internet-facing flagVulnerability priority · exploit-test verdict · business multiplierExposure is 10% of priority and gates reachability

IT Asset Inventory

The asset register

The list of everything you own. This is the foundation — every score in the module ultimately hangs off an asset record.

How assets get here

RouteWhat it means
Manual entrySomeone typed it in. Trustworthy for identity, usually thin on technical detail.
CSV importBulk load from a spreadsheet or another CMDB.
Agent / scan discoveryFound automatically. Rich technical detail, carries a last_seen_at timestamp.
Promoted from softwareAn application found running on a host is promoted to its own asset record. See Software.
Why provenance matters

An asset last seen 200 days ago may not exist any more. Findings against it are noise, and they inflate your risk numbers. Discovery source and last-seen date are how you tell a live estate from a stale one.

Criticality — the most consequential field

Criticality (low medium high critical) feeds vulnerability priority, posture scoring and remediation triggers. Get it wrong and every downstream number is wrong.

It can be set two ways, and this module currently supports both:

  • Derived from CIA ratings — Confidentiality, Integrity and Availability each rated 1–5; the system maps the result to a criticality band.
  • A formal criticality assessment — a reviewed, approved workflow (see Criticality Assessments).
Known gap

These two mechanisms do not currently reconcile with each other. An asset can carry a derived criticality and a formally assessed one that disagree. Treat the derived value as the one the scoring engine actually uses.

Identity & ownership

Owner, custodian, business owner, escalation contact, department, owning team. Nine role fields in total, with a completeness indicator.

Why so many owner fields

In a real incident, "who owns this" has several answers: who is accountable (business owner), who operates it (custodian), who to phone at 3am (escalation contact). Remediation approval is blocked until at least one of these — a person or a department — is set, because approving work nobody has been assigned is an instruction addressed to nobody.

Network, platform, hardware, procurement

Host name, IP, OS family and version, serial number, vendor, location, purchase and EOL dates. Mostly descriptive — but two fields do real work:

  • ip_address — used to group co-located assets. Loopback (127.0.0.1) and link-local addresses are excluded, because every locally-discovered asset carries them and grouping on them merges unrelated machines.
  • os_family — decides which CIS benchmark rules apply, and shapes the remediation instructions generated for a finding.
Asset detail · tab 2

Risk & Controls

The asset's risk score, broken into its parts, plus the controls that cover it and the risks linked to it.

Residual risk card

Shows the composite posture score and a bar for each of the five dimensions. It reads from exactly the same endpoint as the Risk Posture page — one asset, one risk number.

CIA card

Confidentiality, Integrity and Availability, each 1–5. These describe what happens if this asset is compromised, not how likely that is.

RatingConfidentialityIntegrityAvailability
1Public informationErrors are harmlessCan be down indefinitely
3Internal onlyErrors cause reworkHours of downtime tolerable
5Regulated / secretErrors cause financial or legal harmDowntime stops the business
Set vs assumed

If nobody sets these, the system falls back to a value derived from criticality — and marks the CIA dimension as not known, so it is excluded from the score rather than counted as evidence. An asset with guessed CIA has lower data quality, which is the honest outcome.

CIS benchmark card

Summarises hardening-benchmark results. Detail lives on the Compliance tab.

Linked controls

Which of your framework or internal controls cover this asset. Coverage is measured against a target of 12 controls per asset.

coverage % = linked controls ÷ 12 (capped at 100%)
Why 12, and why it's arbitrary on purpose

There is no universal right number of controls per asset. Twelve is a house convention that makes coverage comparable between assets. What matters is that every part of the product uses the same number — an asset that reads 8% here must read 8% everywhere.

Auto-suggested framework mappings

The system proposes controls to link, based on the asset's profile — OS family, vendor, business function, data classification and network segment. Each suggestion carries a confidence band, and you accept or dismiss it.

Why suggest at all: mapping controls to assets by hand across a large estate is the single most tedious task in GRC, and the one most often skipped. Suggestions make it a review task instead of an authoring task. Why they are suggestions and not automatic: a control mapping is an assertion you may have to defend to an auditor. A human accepts it.

If suggestions look empty

Signals are skipped silently when the profile field they depend on is unset. An asset with no OS family, vendor or business function recorded will produce few or no recommendations — the fix is filling in the profile, not re-running the suggester.

Linked risks

Entries from the enterprise risk register that name this asset. This connects technical findings to board-level risk language, which is usually what a GRC purchase is actually for.

Why "accepted forever" is called out

Nobody consciously decides to accept a risk permanently — it's what you get when the review-date field was left blank. Six months later it is invisible and unreviewed. Naming it is the difference between a decision and an oversight.

Asset detail · tab 4

Software

What is installed on this machine, and which of it can be matched to CVEs.

Installed software

Package inventory from discovery — name, version, publisher. Descriptive on its own.

Software identifiers (CPE / PURL)

A CPE is the standard machine-readable name for a product ("cpe:2.3:a:apache:log4j:2.14.1"). It is how NVD describes what a CVE affects. Without a CPE, software cannot be automatically matched to vulnerabilities.

Business translation

"We have Log4j 2.14.1 installed" and "we are affected by Log4Shell" are only connected if something speaks both languages. CPE is that language. An estate with no identifiers can only find vulnerabilities by scanning, never by matching a published advisory against what it already knows.

Promote to asset

Turns a detected application into its own asset record — a child of the host. Use it when the application deserves its own owner, criticality and control mappings (a database engine), and leave it as software when it doesn't (a PDF reader).

The room-and-chair model

The host is the room; applications running on it are the chairs. Both can be assets, and the room's compliance posture is partly a function of what's in it — which is why co-located assets influence each other's CIS scores.

Declared dependencies between assets — your CMDB graph.

Why this matters for security, not just inventory

Relationships drive blast radius — what an attacker reaches next after compromising this asset. They also change the Lateral Movement stage of the exploit assessment.

Empty means unmapped, not safe

An asset with no declared relationships shows an empty blast radius. That is an absence of data, not evidence of isolation. The product says so explicitly rather than implying containment.

Asset detail · tab 6

Compliance — CIS benchmark

Hardening checks. A vulnerability is a defect someone else shipped; a benchmark failure is a configuration you chose.

What a CIS benchmark is

The Center for Internet Security publishes hardening baselines per platform — hundreds of individual checks like "password minimum length ≥ 14" or "SMBv1 disabled". Each check passes, fails, errors, or was never run.

How the score is built

pass rate      = passed ÷ total applicable rules
scanned gap    = failed ÷ (passed + failed)
coverage penalty = never-scanned ÷ total
CIS gap        = 0.8 × scanned gap  +  0.2 × coverage penalty

Two things are being measured at once, deliberately: how much failed, and how much was never checked. An asset with 10 rules passed out of 10 scanned, but 200 rules never run, is not a hardened asset.

SituationResultWhy
No applicable rules in the libraryNot knownNothing to measure — excluded from the score
Rules exist, none ever runNot knownSame reason
Runs exist but every one erroredGap = 1.0 (worst)A broken scan is maximum uncertainty, not a clean bill of health
Mixed pass / failFormula aboveNormal case

Co-located blending

When several assets share a real host address, their compliance is blended — 60% the host's own score, 40% a criticality-weighted average of the applications on it. The room-and-chair model again: a hardened host running an unpatched database is not a hardened environment.

Borrowed scores are labelled

If an asset has never been scanned but its neighbours have, the blended figure is used and flagged as borrowed. It is a defensible signal, but it is not this asset's own evidence and the page must not pretend otherwise.

Asset detail · tab 7

Criticality Assessments

The formal, reviewed version of "how much does this asset matter".

Two assessment types are supported:

  • ISCA — Information System Criticality Assessment. Rates the system as a whole.
  • IACA — Information Asset Criticality Assessment. Rates the data the system holds.

Each produces a score, a band, and a status (draft, submitted, approved). Approved assessments are the ones an auditor will ask to see.

Why a formal workflow exists alongside a simple field

The CIA sliders let an engineer record a working judgement in ten seconds. A regulator wants something else: a documented assessment, with a named approver and a date. Both are legitimate; they serve different audiences.

Known gap

The formal assessment and the derived criticality do not reconcile. The scoring engine uses the derived value. Where they disagree, nothing currently flags it.

Asset detail · tabs 8–12

Lifecycle, Attachments, Notes, History, Trajectory

Lifecycle

Where the asset sits in its life: requested → procured → deployed → active → maintenance → retired → disposed. Plus warranty and EOL dates.

Why security cares

An asset past end-of-life stops receiving vendor patches. At that point "apply the vendor patch" is no longer available as a remediation, and every finding on it becomes a compensating-control or replacement decision.

Note

State changes update the asset in place; there is no transition history table, so there is no record of who changed a lifecycle state or when.

Attachments

Evidence documents linked to the asset — architecture diagrams, approvals, scan reports. In an audit, an assertion without an artefact behind it is just a claim.

Notes

Free-text, attributed and timestamped. For context that doesn't fit a field: "scheduled for decommission Q3, do not remediate".

History

The audit trail — who changed which field, when. Populated from the moment journalling was enabled; assets edited before then show nothing.

Trajectory

A graph view: asset → vulnerability → business risk. The same records as the Vulnerabilities and Risk & Controls tabs, drawn as a chain rather than a list. Useful for explaining a path to a non-technical audience.

Risk Posture

What risk posture is

One number, 0–100, summarising how exposed an asset is. Higher is worse.

The dashboard shows the portfolio: an average score, a band distribution, and every asset ranked. Clicking through gives the per-asset breakdown.

Bands

ScoreBandMeans
0–25containedHealthy posture
25–50watchWatch list
50–75elevatedRemediate soon
75–100severeImmediate action
Why these words and not low/medium/high

Asset criticality already uses low/medium/high/critical. Reusing those words for posture produced sentences like "a high asset with high risk", which nobody could parse. Different concepts get different vocabulary.

Risk Posture

The five dimensions

Each scores 0–1 where 1 is worst. They are weighted, summed, and expressed out of 100.

DimensionWeightMeasuresSource
Vulnerabilities30%The worst effective risk among open findingsLinked vulnerabilities
CIS benchmark25%Hardening failures + unscanned coverageCompliance scan runs
CIA value15%How much damage compromise causesC/I/A ratings
Control coverage15%Controls not covering this assetControl links ÷ 12
Linked risks15%Open entries in the risk registerRisk-asset links

Why vulnerabilities carry the most weight

It is the only dimension describing something an attacker can act on today. Control coverage and CIA describe preparedness and consequence; a weaponised CVE on an internet-facing box describes an opportunity.

Why the vulnerability dimension takes the maximum, not the sum

An attacker needs one way in. An asset with one critical finding is not safer than an asset with twenty medium ones just because the arithmetic is smaller. The dimension takes the worst effective risk among open findings.

Risk Posture

Known vs unknown — the most important idea here

A dimension with no evidence behind it is excluded, not scored as zero.

Scoring a missing dimension as 0 would mean "we have no scan data" produced the same result as "everything passed". So each dimension carries a known flag, and the composite is normalised over only the known ones:

score = Σ(weight × dimension score) for known dimensions
        ────────────────────────────────────────────────
                 Σ(weight) for known dimensions

Data quality

The share of total weight that is actually known. An asset scored on vulnerabilities and CIA alone (30% + 15%) has 45% data quality.

DimensionUnknown when
CISNo applicable rules, or no runs against them
VulnerabilitiesNo findings linked to the asset at all
CIANo ratings set and no criticality to derive from
Control coverageNo controls linked
Linked risksNo risk-register entries reference the asset
Read the score and the data quality together

A brand-new asset with nothing recorded can show a low score and a reassuring band, because it is being judged on one or two dimensions. A low score at 20% data quality means "we don't know", not "it's fine." This is the single most common way to misread this page.

Vulnerability detail · tab 2

Remediation

What are we doing about it? Three groups, because there are only three possible answers.

1 · The fix→ 2 · Who owns it→ 3 · If we're not fixing it yet

Group 1 — The fix

Why this needs action now

The red flags that justified acting, each naming its evidence. If none fire, the panel says so — the finding waits for the normal patch cycle. See What starts remediation.

The remediation plan

A concrete fix: title, type (patch / config / IaC), a copyable artefact of the actual steps, and a rationale. Generated from the finding's context — CVE, asset type, OS family, exposure.

A draft is shown immediately without being saved. Adopting it is the moment of intent, because a saved plan carries an approval trail and approvals should attach to text someone chose.

The four-stage lifecycle

StageAdvanced byRecordsGate
RecommendedAdopting the planThe plan text, and why it was warranted—
ApprovedA human clickWho authorised it, when, change windowRequires an owner
AppliedA human clickAn execution log of the stepsAuto-apply refused on critical assets
VerifiedA human clickAn attestation with evidenceRequires evidence text
Two things this stage model does not claim

"Applied" does not patch anything. Execution runs through a simulated executor that records the steps without touching a host. Connecting a real executor is a deliberate, separate decision.

"Verified" does not re-scan. It records that a named person, on a named date, attested the fix is in place — and what they offered as grounds. Evidence text is mandatory, because a confirmation with nothing behind it looks like proof in an audit export.

Patch information

Vendor advisories and KB articles from PSIRT feeds. When a lookup finds nothing, it says so and dates it — "no vendor patch published" is the fact that decides whether compensating controls are your only option.

Group 2 — Who owns it

Department assignment, with its own SLA override and escalation path. Distinct from the individual assignee in the sidebar: one routes to a person, one to a queue. Either satisfies the approval gate.

Group 3 — If we're not fixing it yet

Mitigations (compensating controls)

Tracked tasks with owner, priority, status, target date and effort. The plan is the fix; mitigations are the work, and specifically the work that reduces exposure without removing the flaw — segmentation, WAF rules, monitoring, access restriction.

Suggest compensating controls

AI proposes candidate mitigations from the finding's context. It is explicitly forbidden from proposing the patch, from asserting whether a patch exists, and from setting deadlines — those belong to the plan and the SLA. Accept a suggestion and it becomes a tracked mitigation.

Exception workflow

The formal "we are deliberately not fixing this" decision.

none → requested → approved → expired
                  ↘ denied → requested
       approved → revoked (terminal)

An approved exception carries a review date. When it passes, the exception expires and the finding reopens — because lapsing on paper while staying invisible in every queue is the worst of both outcomes.

Remediate vs mitigate vs accept

Remediation — the flaw is gone. Mitigation — the flaw is still there, you put a guard on it. Acceptance — the flaw is still there and you've decided to live with it, on the record, with a date. These are the only three answers, which is why the tab has exactly three groups.

Vulnerability detail · tab 3

Exploit Test

Could this actually be reached and used against this asset — and how far would it spread?

Nothing is executed

This assessment reads stored evidence only. No scanner, exploit or payload is run against any host. Every verdict is a derivation from data already held, and the page says so.

Group 1 — Can it be reached?

The MITRE ATT&CK kill chain

ATT&CK is the industry catalogue of attacker techniques. Six stages are mapped, each marked reached (evidence says yes), possible (can't rule out) or blocked (something upstream stops it). Clicking a stage explains why it is in that state and what would stop it.

StageTechniqueQuestion
ReconnaissanceT1595Can an attacker find this asset?
Initial accessT1190Can they get in through this flaw?
ExecutionT1203Can they run code?
Privilege escalationT1068Can they become admin?
Lateral movementT1210Can they reach other assets?
ImpactT1005What can they take or break?

The evidence table

One row per fact: what we know, and what would block it. The five checks are always the same questions; the answers come from this finding and its asset.

CheckSource
Internet-facingAsset record
Network attack vectorThe AV: field of the CVSS vector
Known-exploited / public exploitCISA KEV + public exploit count
Patch available but unappliedVendor patch refs + finding status
Network segmentationAdjacent asset count
These blockers are heuristics

They are derived from the same evidence, inverted — they are not linked to your ISO or NIST control library. A "blocked" step here is not yet usable as control evidence.

Group 2 — How far would it spread?

Blast radius: assets reachable next, each labelled by how we know — a declared relationship (a stated fact) or a shared address (our inference). Vulnerability chain: prerequisite findings that must also exist for this one to be exploitable, and findings that depend on this one.

Why prerequisites change urgency

A privilege-escalation flaw on a server is far more urgent when a remote-code-execution flaw lets an attacker reach that server in the first place. Fixing the RCE makes the escalation flaw academic. Chains let you fix one thing and close several.

Group 3 — Prove it

The retest log. A human records date, tester, result (pass / fail / partial), findings and evidence. Everything above is derivation; this is the only part that is proof. Until a retest exists, remediation is claimed but not demonstrated.

Vulnerability detail · tabs 4–5

History & Notes

History

The audit trail: every status change, assignment, plan adoption, approval, application and attestation — with actor and timestamp.

Why this is the tab an auditor opens first

An auditor rarely disputes that you fixed something. They ask who decided, when, and on what basis. A product that changes state without recording those three things cannot answer, however good its remediation workflow looks.

Notes

Free-text, attributed, timestamped. For reasoning that must outlive a status field: why an exception was granted, what the vendor said, why this is a false positive.

Notes are deliberately separate from history. History is what the system observed; notes are what a person chose to record.

Scoring

How vulnerability priority is calculated

Seven weighted signals, 0–10 internally, displayed ×10 as N/100.

SignalWeightWhy it's in
CVSS severity20%How bad the flaw is if used
EPSS probability20%How likely anyone is to use it
Exploit maturity15%How ready-made the attack is
CISA KEV15%Whether it is being used right now
Attack vector10%How reachable the flaw is
Internet exposure10%Whether the host faces outward
Asset criticality10%How much the host matters

Why CVSS is only 20%

CVSS is a static severity rating. Two findings with identical CVSS can differ enormously in real risk depending on whether an exploit is weaponised, whether the flaw is network-reachable, and whether the host faces the internet. Sixty percent of the weight sits on exploitability and context for that reason.

The KEV floor

Anything on CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list is floored at 80/100 regardless of everything else. A bug being actively used in the wild is never a medium priority, whatever the arithmetic says.

A worked example

signal                value/10   weight   points
CVSS severity            7.100     0.20    1.420
EPSS probability         0.045     0.20    0.009
Exploit maturity        10.000     0.15    1.500
CISA KEV                 0.000     0.15    0.000
Attack vector            5.000     0.10    0.500
Internet exposure        0.000     0.10    0.000
Asset criticality        7.000     0.10    0.700
                                  TOTAL    4.129   →  41 / 100

Reading it: the score is driven by CVSS and exploit maturity. EPSS contributes almost nothing because 0.5% is genuinely low. Attack vector contributed 0.5 points from an assumed mid value, because no CVSS vector was stored — which is why unknown fields are worth chasing.

Scoring

Exploit availability and "weaponised"

The rule you asked about: if an exploit exists, what changes — and how do we know it exists at all?

The maturity ladder

MaturityAssigned whenSignal valuePoints (of 15)
WeaponisedOn CISA KEV, or more than 2 public exploit repos1.015.0
FunctionalA working exploit exists but isn't packaged0.710.5
Proof of concept1–2 public repos0.46.0
UnprovenNo public exploit found0.11.5
UnknownNever enriched0.34.5
Unknown is not zero

A CVE nobody has assessed still carries latent risk. Scoring it 0 would rank an unexamined finding below one confirmed harmless. It sits at 0.3 — below proof-of-concept, above unproven.

How we find out whether an exploit exists

Two independent sources:

  1. CISA KEV — an authoritative government list of CVEs confirmed exploited in the wild. Binary and trustworthy. KEV alone is enough for weaponised.
  2. Public exploit repositories — a GitHub search for repositories named after the CVE.
Why the search is narrow on purpose

Searching names, descriptions and readmes matches every CVE catalogue, scanner and "awesome-security" list on GitHub — a CVE that does not exist returned 258 hits that way, which then read as 258 public exploits and stamped the finding weaponised.

Repositories that genuinely target a CVE almost always name themselves after it; catalogues do not. Measured on the same two CVEs: name-only search gives Log4Shell 183 and a nonexistent CVE 0. Aggregators are filtered out of the displayed list on top of that.

So: if an exploit is available, does priority change?

Yes, substantially. Moving from unproven to weaponised swings 13.5 points of the 100. Add KEV and it also swings the 15-point KEV signal and triggers the 80-point floor.

The business version

A flaw only a national intelligence service could exploit is a different problem from one with a working script on GitHub. The first is a patching-cycle item; the second is reachable by anyone who can copy and paste. Exploit maturity is how the system tells those apart — and it is why two findings with identical CVSS can sit at opposite ends of your queue.

Scoring

What actually starts remediation

Not the score. Six independent red flags, each naming a specific fact.

Priority ranks the queue. It does not decide that something needs work today. That is done by red flags — and if none fire, the finding waits for the normal patch cycle.

Why raw facts and not the blended score

"41/100" tells you where something sits in a list. It does not tell you what is wrong. "183 public exploits" and "6 days past its due date" are actionable — you can act on each independently, and you can argue with each independently. A blended number hides both.

The emergency-room analogy

Every patient gets a triage score deciding who is seen first — that's priority. But some things take you straight through regardless of your score: chest pain, bleeding. Those are red flags. A finding can score modestly and still jump the queue because working exploit code is public and the deadline has passed.

An empty flag list is a real answer, not missing data. A CVSS 9.8 that is not due yet, has no published exploit, low EPSS, and sits on an internal non-critical box raises nothing — correctly. It goes into the normal patch cycle.

Four external feeds and your own records. Nothing in the scoring is invented.

Vendor PSIRT

Your estate
assets · scans · controls · risks

Criticality, exposure, CIA, control links, CIS runs — the context that makes a CVE yours.

Enrichment is pulled, not pushed

External data is fetched when a finding is enriched — on demand or on a refresh cycle. A CVE added to KEV this morning does not change your scores until enrichment next runs. The Analysis tab shows when a finding was last enriched, and flags a stored score that has drifted from current evidence.

Practical limits

NVD rate-limits unauthenticated callers to roughly 5 requests per 30 seconds — set an API key before bulk enrichment. Findings using non-public or internal CVE identifiers will legitimately return nothing from every feed; empty is the correct answer, not a failure.

Honest limitations

What this module does not currently do. Worth knowing before you present it.

LimitationWhat it means in practice
Applying a fix is simulatedNo command is executed on any host. The lifecycle records intent and steps; a human still carries out the work.
Verification is an attestationNothing re-scans. A named person asserts the fix is in place and supplies evidence.
Exploit-test blockers aren't control-mappedThey are heuristics, so a "blocked" step is not yet usable as control evidence.
Two criticality mechanismsDerived-from-CIA and the formal assessment do not reconcile. Scoring uses the derived value.
No lifecycle transition historyAsset lifecycle states change in place, with no record of who or when.
Enrichment is on demandScores reflect the last enrichment, not this minute. Stored-vs-current drift is flagged on the Analysis tab.
Empty ≠ safeEmpty blast radius, 0% coverage and unknown dimensions all mean unmeasured. Read data quality alongside every score.
The principle behind all of these

Where the system does not know something, it is designed to say so rather than render a confident-looking number. An absent value is recoverable; a wrong value that looks authoritative is not.
