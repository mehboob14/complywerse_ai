The one-minute version

A scanner tells you there are 4,000 problems. This module turns that into an ordered list with owners, deadlines and evidence — and can prove months later who decided what.

The narrowing is the product. Anyone can list 4,000 problems; the value is defending which twelve you chose.

Three questions, three parts

PartAnswers
IT Asset InventoryWhat do we own, who owns it, how much does it matter?
VulnerabilitiesWhat is wrong with it, and how urgent is each one?
Risk PosturePutting both together — how exposed is this asset?
If you remember one thing

Severity is not priority. A CVSS 9.8 with no published exploit, on an internal test box, behind a patch you already applied, is not your most urgent problem. Severity describes the flaw. Priority describes your exposure to it.

How it thinks

Where the data comes from

Four public feeds describe the flaw. Your own records describe what it means to you. Nothing is invented.

Enrichment is pulled, not pushed. A CVE added to KEV this morning does not change your scores until enrichment next runs — and the page flags when a stored score has drifted from current evidence.

Why this matters in a demo

Every number can be traced to a named public source. When someone asks "where did 41 come from", the answer is a chain of citable facts, not a black box.

How it thinks

How a finding is scored

Seven signals, weighted. CVSS is deliberately only a fifth of it.

Two findings with identical CVSS can differ enormously in real risk, depending on whether an exploit is weaponised, whether the flaw is network-reachable, and whether the host faces the internet.

A worked example

signal                value/10   weight   points
CVSS severity            7.100     0.20    1.420
EPSS probability         0.045     0.20    0.009
Exploit maturity        10.000     0.15    1.500
CISA KEV                 0.000     0.15    0.000
Attack vector            5.000     0.10    0.500   ← assumed, no vector stored
Internet exposure        0.000     0.10    0.000
Asset criticality        7.000     0.10    0.700
                                  TOTAL    4.129   →  41 / 100
The KEV floor

Anything on CISA's actively-exploited list is floored at 80/100 regardless of the arithmetic. A bug being used in the wild right now is never a medium priority.

How it thinks

Exploits and "weaponised"

Whether ready-made attack code exists changes everything — so how we decide that has to be defensible.

How we know an exploit exists

Two independent sources: CISA KEV (authoritative, binary — enough on its own for weaponised), and public repositories named after the CVE.

Why the repository search is narrow on purpose

Searching names, descriptions and readmes matches every CVE catalogue and security link-list on GitHub. A CVE that does not exist returned 258 hits that way — which then read as 258 public exploits and stamped the finding weaponised.

Repositories that genuinely target a CVE almost always name themselves after it; catalogues do not. Measured on the same two CVEs: name-only search gives Log4Shell 183 and a nonexistent CVE 0.

What actually triggers action

Not the score. Six red flags, each naming a specific fact.

The emergency-room analogy

Every patient gets a triage score deciding who is seen first — that is priority. But some things take you straight through regardless: chest pain, bleeding. Those are red flags. A finding can score modestly and still jump the queue.

An empty list is a real answer

A CVSS 9.8 that is not due yet, has no published exploit, low EPSS, and sits on an internal non-critical box raises nothing — correctly. It goes into the normal patch cycle. That restraint is what stops a queue from being 4,000 items of equal urgency.

How work gets done

The remediation lifecycle

Four stages, each recording who and when — so "we fixed it" becomes evidence rather than a claim.

Two claims this deliberately does not make

"Applied" does not patch anything. A real executor is a separate, deliberate decision — it must not be something you get by accident.

"Verified" does not re-scan. It records that a named person, on a named date, asserted the fix is in place — and what they offered as grounds. Evidence text is mandatory, because a confirmation with nothing behind it looks like proof in an audit export.

Why the gates exist

Approval without an owner is an instruction addressed to nobody — a plan can sit approved, breach its deadline, and no one was ever asked. Verification without evidence is the more dangerous one: it manufactures something that reads like proof.

How work gets done

Accepting risk — and why it must expire

There are only three answers to a finding, and the third one is the one organisations get wrong.

AnswerWhat happens to the flaw
RemediateGone. You fixed it.
MitigateStill there. You put a guard on it — segmentation, WAF, monitoring.
AcceptStill there, and you have decided to live with it — on the record, with a date.
The failure mode this prevents

An acceptance without a review date is accepted forever — and nobody consciously decides that. It is what you get when a field was left blank. Six months later it is invisible and unreviewed, and the risk is being carried with no current sign-off. The system flags undated acceptances by name.

Asset risk posture

Five dimensions, one score — and a confidence figure that matters as much as the score.

Business impact inputs

Customer-facing (1.2×), internet-facing (1.3×), regulated data (1.4×) and operational dependency (0.8×–1.5×) are multipliers. They are the point where business context enters a technical scoring system — nobody can scan for "this holds regulated data". It is why the same CVE ranks differently across your estate.

Straight talk

What is real, and what is not

Say this before someone discovers it. Credibility is cheaper to keep than to rebuild.

Working today

  • The scoring engine, fed by live public data — NVD, FIRST, CISA and GitHub are genuinely connected and verified against real CVEs.
  • The full remediation lifecycle, with its approval and evidence gates.
  • Exception handling with real expiry — a lapsed acceptance reopens the finding.
  • The audit trail behind every state change.

The honest summary

The machine is built and correct. It is currently grinding demo data. That is why so much reads as empty — 0% coverage, empty blast radius, "no vendor patch published". Those are accurate answers about an estate that does not exist yet, not defects.

What connecting one real scanner would prove

Right now we know the scoring is arithmetically correct. We do not yet know it is sensible. Point it at one real subnet and within a day you learn:

  • Do the priorities match what your engineers would have picked by hand?
  • Is 12 controls the right coverage target, or does every real asset sit at 4?
  • Does "more than two exploit repos means weaponised" hold up against real CVEs?
  • Do the risk bands spread sensibly, or does everything land in "watch"?

Those are calibration questions, and no amount of demo data answers them. That is the last real gap — not features, calibration against reality.
